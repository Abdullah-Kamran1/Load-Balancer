# Final report — Phase 9

**Project:** educational asyncio L7 HTTP reverse proxy vs HAProxy 3.4.7.
**Scope:** Phases 0–8 produced the implementation and dataset; this report
answers the ten questions in `load_balancer_project_plan.md` (lines 569-580)
and points to the evidence for every claim.

## 1. Methodology and configuration

- **Dataset:** 18 baseline cases (custom/HAProxy × 3 algorithms ×
  50/200/500 clients), 3 × 60 s runs per case, median reported; plus 6
  failure/recovery cases (50 clients, backend-2 killed at t=30 s).
  Raw wrk outputs, snapshots, resource samples and timelines are kept under
  `results/raw/`; tracked summaries are in `results/structured/`.
- **Fixed conditions:** path/payload `GET /work?delay_ms=25`, same four
  unequal-capacity backends (25 ms/16, 100 ms/8, 300 ms/4, 50 ms/6 slots),
  one LB at a time, 10 s warm-up, LB restart before each measured run, same
  machine/Docker/versions. See [benchmark_matrix.md](benchmark_matrix.md),
  [haproxy_setup.md](haproxy_setup.md) and
  [metric_definitions.md](metric_definitions.md).
- **Environment:** 12th Gen i7-12700H, 20 logical CPUs, 15.7 GiB RAM,
  Python 3.15.2, Docker 29.9.3, HAProxy 3.4.7-8872a26 (pinned by digest).
  Exact hashes/versions: `results/structured/benchmark_environment.txt` and the
  per-run `haproxy_environment_<run_id>.txt` files.
- **Figure provenance:** every number quoted below is generated into
  `results/structured/report_data.csv` (columns `metric, mode, strategy,
  clients, value, evidence`) by `scripts/build_report_data.py`; plots are
  generated by `scripts/build_charts.py` and `scripts/build_report_plots.py`.
- **Statistical treatment:** medians of three runs; wrk-side percentiles are
  the cross-implementation latency source because HAProxy's stats expose
  averages, not distributions.

## 3. Answers to the plan's ten questions

### 1. Does round robin distribute requests evenly?

Yes. HAProxy `roundrobin` gave exactly 25% to each backend at all three
concurrencies (max deviation 0.00 pp). Custom `round_robin` deviated by 3.36 pp
at 50 clients, 0.92 pp at 200 and 0.00 pp at 500. Minor short-run deviations
come from in-flight requests and the one-connection-per-request model, not
from selection bias. Evidence: `report_data.csv`
(`round_robin_evenness_pct`), [backend_shares.svg](../results/plots/backend_shares.svg),
`results/structured/backends.csv`.

### 2. Does even distribution produce good performance with unequal backends?

No. Custom round robin at 50 clients delivered the highest RPS (943) but also
the most 5xx (49,388) and the worst p99 (647 ms) among the custom strategies,
because it sends a fixed quarter of requests to the 300 ms/4-slot backend.
Least-active and sticky strategies produced fewer error responses and lower
tail latency at roughly half the throughput. HAProxy round robin similarly
returned slightly more RPS than leastconn (3,698 vs 3,585) with comparable
p99 (36.3 vs 35.4 ms), but both pushed the farm into saturation. Conclusion:
**evenness is not the same as good performance** when capacity is unequal.
Evidence: [rps_by_load.svg](../results/plots/rps_by_load.svg),
[p99_by_load.svg](../results/plots/p99_by_load.svg), `baseline_medians.csv`.

### 3. Does least active requests improve latency or throughput for unequal backends?

It improves tail latency and reduces errors at the cost of raw throughput.
At 50 clients the custom proxy's p99 fell from 647 ms (round robin) to 560 ms
(least active) and 5xx from 49,388 to 21,302, while RPS fell from 943 to 519.
HAProxy `leastconn` was nearly identical to `roundrobin` on p99 (35.4 vs 36.3 ms)
and was *less* even at 50 clients (30.46% max share / 14.47 pp deviation)
because it counts server-side connections and queued work, not in-flight HTTP
requests. The two algorithms are not equivalent; see question 9. Evidence:
`report_data.csv`, `docs/haproxy_setup.md` (algorithm semantics table).

### 4. How does IP hash affect distribution and repeatability?

It maximizes stickiness and repeatability but concentrates load. A single test
client IP was pinned to one backend in the Phase 4 live check (40/40 responses
from backend-1, `results/raw/phase4_live_ip_hash.txt`). In the matrix, custom
`ip_hash` reached 63.62% max share at 50 clients and 96.96% at 500; HAProxy
`source` pinned 100% to backend-2 at every concurrency. The price is tail
latency under load: custom p99 558 ms at 50 and 1,960 ms at 500; HAProxy source
p99 51.6 ms at 50 (still the worst of its three strategies). Evidence:
[backend_shares.svg](../results/plots/backend_shares.svg), `baseline_medians.csv`.

### 5. What happens to IP-hash distribution when a backend is removed?

The mapping is `SHA-256(client_ip) mod len(healthy_backends)`
(`load_balancer/algorithms/ip_hash.py`). Changing the healthy set changes the
modulus, so **some clients are remapped**; the unit test
`test_unhealthy_backend_not_selected_and_mapping_shifts`
(`load_balancer/tests/test_algorithms.py`) shows one IP moving from backend `c`
to `a`/`b` when `c` becomes unhealthy, and a 50-IP test never selects an
unhealthy backend. HAProxy's `source` carries the same caveat in its manual:
a client keeps its server "as long as no server goes down or up". The failure
runs confirmed re-admission after backend-2 restarted, but did not log
per-client remapping timestamps, so the distribution shift is established by
code/test/semantics rather than a plotted curve. Evidence:
`docs/haproxy_setup.md` (algorithm section), `test_algorithms.py`.

### 6. How long does each load balancer take to detect a backend failure?

Detection (kill → first DOWN after the kill) was **4.72–5.94 s for the custom
proxy** and **4.42–4.79 s for HAProxy** with three failures, a 1 s interval and
1 s check timeout. On this Windows host a connect to a killed container is
silently dropped, so every failed probe consumes `timeout + interval`;
HAProxy's check phase adds up to one interval of jitter. Both implementations
therefore behave as their configuration predicts, and neither is faster by a
wide margin. Evidence: `results/structured/failures.csv`,
[failure_timeline.svg](../results/plots/failure_timeline.svg),
`docs/haproxy_setup.md` (transitional `UP 2/3` states).

### 7. How many requests fail during the health-check detection window?

From the kill to the first DOWN the custom proxy recorded **158–366** 5xx
responses, HAProxy **1,678–10,488**. The absolute counts are not directly
comparable: HAProxy offered roughly 3,600 rps versus the custom proxy's few
hundred, so it had far more in-flight requests aimed at the dying server.
Over the full 30 s after the kill the counts were 11,410–23,460 (custom) and
38,550–57,196 (HAProxy), dominated by capacity 503s; the detection-window
figure is the closer analogue of "failures caused by the failure".
Evidence: `failures.csv` (`failed_detection`, `failed_after_kill_30s`),
[failure_detection_window.svg](../results/plots/failure_detection_window.svg),
[failure_requests.svg](../results/plots/failure_requests.svg).

### 8. Does HTTP keep-alive change throughput, latency, or connection counts?

Yes, substantially for the client side. Phase 3's paired 8 s runs: keep-alive
**3,402 requests / 424.77 rps / 23.83 ms p50** versus close-mode
**2,721 requests / 339.83 rps / 27.31 ms p50** — **1.25× throughput** and
**3.48 ms lower p50**. Connection-state observation showed ~340 requests per
TCP connection with `LISTEN`/no `TIME_WAIT`, versus 128 sockets in `TIME_WAIT`
right after close-mode. The custom proxy still opens **one upstream
connection per request**; upstream pooling is an optional extension, and the
~25% client-side gain is a lower bound on what reuse could buy. Evidence:
`results/raw/phase3_wrk_keepalive.txt`, `phase3_wrk_close.txt`,
`phase3_conn_states.txt`, `report_data.csv` (`keepalive_rps_ratio`,
`keepalive_p50_delta_ms`, `requests_per_connection`),
`load_balancer/tests/test_persistent_connection.py`.

### 9. Why can active TCP connections differ from active HTTP requests?

A persistent connection can carry hundreds of sequential requests, and can
also be idle between them. In one wrk run the custom snapshot recorded
`connections_total = 50` against `requests_total = 59,109` (~1,182 requests
per connection); HAProxy's frontend row similarly showed 11 client
connections for 11,145 requests. HAProxy `leastconn` counts *connections*
(including queued work), while the custom `least_active_requests` counts
*in-flight requests* on the backend objects; an idle keep-alive connection
inflates the former without representing work. Under `option http-server-close`
a server connection ≈ one request in this harness, but the definitions are
not identical. Evidence: `report_data.csv`
(`snapshot_requests_per_connection`), `results/structured/combined.csv`,
`docs/haproxy_setup.md` (counters and semantics), `docs/metric_definitions.md`.

### 10. Where does the custom proxy fall behind HAProxy, and why?

At 50 clients the custom proxy managed 360–943 rps with 558–647 ms p99,
while HAProxy managed 1,785–3,698 rps with 36–52 ms p99; custom CPU was
16–87% of one core, HAProxy 56–204% across cores. Causes, in order of
importance: (1) HAProxy is mature C code with an event-driven multi-threaded
core; the custom proxy is single-threaded asyncio + pure-Python h11 framing.
(2) The custom proxy opens one upstream connection per request; upstream
pooling is explicitly deferred. (3) Python object/GC and per-request latency
sampling add overhead. (4) Different accounting: HAProxy runs in a container
and its 5xx/CPU come from different sources (documented). The keep-alive
experiment (question 8) quantifies one concrete piece of the gap; the rest is
architectural, not an algorithm defect. Evidence:
[cpu_comparison.svg](../results/plots/cpu_comparison.svg),
[rps_by_load.svg](../results/plots/rps_by_load.svg),
[benchmark_matrix.md](benchmark_matrix.md#interpretation-limits).

## 5. Limitations

- **p95:** wrk `--latency` emits p50/p75/p90/p99 only. The plan's p95 has no
  common client-side source; custom snapshots hold a proxy-internal p95 but
  HAProxy has no equivalent. p90 is reported as the closest client percentile.
- **Saturation:** the four-backend farm has 34 concurrency slots, so wrk
  exceeds capacity at every tested concurrency. RPS largely measures how fast
  503s are returned; conclusions are conditional on this regime.
- **CPU:** custom values are self-reported `process_time()` deltas (Windows
  reports zero via `Get-Process`/WMI for spawned processes); HAProxy/wrk use
  `docker stats`. Units are both "% of one core" but accounting differs.
- **Failure timeline:** event markers, not a continuous series; raw
  per-request failure timestamps were not recorded.
- **Sample size:** three runs per case (plan minimum); five were deferred.
- **Non-equivalent algorithms:** `leastconn` vs `least_active_requests` and
  `source` vs SHA-256 `ip_hash` are compared behaviorally, not as equivalents.
- **Historical answers:** questions 5 and 8 rely on Phase 3/4 evidence under
  their original conditions rather than new runs incomparable with Phase 8.
- **No upstream pooling, weights, draining, TLS or dashboards:** these are
  optional extensions in the plan, not part of the core deliverable.

## 6. Conclusions

Round robin is evenly fair but not performance-optimal with unequal backends;
least-active/leastconn trade some throughput for better tail latency and fewer
errors, while IP-hash/source buy repeatability at the cost of concentrated
load and worse tails. Both implementations detect a killed backend in about
4–6 s with three checks, and re-admit it after recovery. The custom proxy's
remaining gap to HAProxy is architectural (single-threaded Python, no upstream
pooling, per-request connection setup), not a failure of its algorithms. The
highest-value next steps are the plan's optional extensions: upstream
connection pooling, capacity-aware weights, consistent hashing, and
connection draining.
